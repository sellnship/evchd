Owning an electric scooter in Chandigarh does add to your electricity bill. That part is straightforward. The more useful question — and the one most buyers do not think to ask — is whether that addition is large enough to push your household into a higher tariff slab, and what that would actually cost you. The answer depends on where your household already sits on Chandigarh's tiered domestic tariff. This article works through that math honestly.

## How much electricity does charging actually use

A typical low-speed electric scooter has a battery that draws around 2 units (kWh) for a full charge. Per the verified charging data for this class, a full charge costs roughly Rs 10 at an all-in rate of around Rs 5 per unit — which works out to approximately Rs 0.17 per km over a rated range of around 60 km or more. Real-world range runs somewhat lower than the brochure figure, call it around 78 per cent of rated range as a rule of thumb, but the energy consumed per charge stays the same regardless.

If you charge every single day, you add around 60 units per month to your household consumption. If you charge every other day — which covers most city commutes — that addition is around 30 units per month.

Those numbers matter because Chandigarh's tariff is not a flat rate.

---

## Chandigarh's JERC tariff: the slab structure you need to understand

Under the JERC five-year tariff order effective 1 November 2025 (issued by the Joint Electricity Regulatory Commission following the privatisation of supply to Chandigarh Power Distribution Ltd), domestic consumers in Chandigarh pay on a tiered basis. The figures below are per the JERC tariff order:

- **Lowest slab:** around Rs 2.75 per unit (modest household consumption)
- **Mid slab (151–400 units per month):** around Rs 4.80 per unit
- **Upper slab (above 400 units per month):** around Rs 5.40 per unit

There is also a fixed charge of approximately Rs 20–30 per kW per month, plus a small electricity duty per unit. When you factor those in, an honest all-in basis for running-cost calculations comes out near Rs 5 per unit for a typical household — not the lowest slab rate alone.

The lowest slab is genuinely cheaper, and that is worth noting. But for running-cost purposes, using only the bottom slab rate understates what most households actually pay.

## The slab-nudge question: who is actually at risk

This is the part that a generic electricity-cost answer will not give you, and it is the real question for Chandigarh households.

Consider a household that currently consumes, say, 130 units a month. It sits comfortably inside the lowest slab. Now add daily EV charging at around 60 units per month. Total consumption climbs to around 190 units — past the 150-unit threshold and into the mid slab at around Rs 4.80 per unit.

The consequence is not just that you pay more for the 60 EV units. Under a tiered system, once you cross the slab boundary, the higher rate applies to your consumption above that threshold. The marginal cost of each additional unit in the mid slab is around Rs 4.80, not Rs 2.75.

Here is a rough illustration using the JERC tariff figures:

**Scenario: light-use household, currently 130 units/month**

Without EV charging, most of that consumption sits in the lowest slab. Add 60 units of daily EV charging:
- The first 20 or so additional units (up to the 150-unit boundary) remain at the lower slab rate
- The remaining roughly 40 units land in the mid slab at around Rs 4.80 per unit

The incremental monthly cost of those 60 EV units works out to somewhere between Rs 150 and Rs 250 in this scenario, depending on the exact fixed-charge split. That is the real monthly bill impact, not simply 60 units multiplied by the lowest slab rate.

**Who is genuinely at risk of a slab jump**

The households that need to pay attention are those already sitting close to a slab boundary:

- A household at around 120–148 units per month, charging daily, will almost certainly cross into the mid slab
- A household at around 360–395 units per month, charging daily, is at risk of nudging past 400 units into the upper slab at around Rs 5.40 per unit

If you are comfortably inside a slab — say, a household at 80 units per month — even daily EV charging will not push you to the 150-unit boundary immediately, and the bill impact is modest.

**Alternate-day charging as a practical buffer**

For households near a slab boundary, charging every other day rather than daily keeps the EV addition to around 30 units per month instead of 60. That alone can be enough to stay within the current slab. It is worth checking your last few electricity bills before you commit to a charging routine.

## Mohali and Panchkula: the comparison

If you are in Mohali (covered by PSPCL, Punjab), the domestic tariff sits at around Rs 5.40 per unit for the first 300 units — there is less of a low-slab cushion. In Panchkula (UHBVN, Haryana), the domestic tariff is broadly comparable to that level as well.

For Mohali and Panchkula residents, the slab-nudge concern is less acute in one sense — there is less distance to fall before hitting the higher rate — but the running cost per unit is also higher from the start. Using around Rs 5 per unit as the all-in basis for the Tricity as a whole is a fair, slightly conservative working figure.

## Putting it together: is the bill impact meaningful

At around Rs 5 per unit all-in, 60 units of monthly EV consumption costs around Rs 300 per month — that is the ceiling if you charge daily and your marginal rate sits at Rs 5. In the lowest Chandigarh slab, the same 60 units would cost around Rs 165. The slab-nudge effect can add a further Rs 50–100 in certain households near a boundary, as illustrated above.

Compare that against what the same distance would cost in petrol: per verified charging-cost data, a full charge covers around 60 km rated range (real-world will be somewhat less), against Rs 150–200 in petrol for the same distance. Even accounting for the slab effect, the running-cost advantage of an electric scooter is substantial. The question this article is answering is not whether EVs are economical — they are — but whether the bill impact is larger than buyers expect, and for light-use households near a slab boundary, it can be.

## What to check before you buy

- Pull out your last three electricity bills and note your average monthly units consumed
- Identify which slab you currently sit in under the JERC tariff
- Add 60 units (daily charging) or 30 units (alternate-day charging) and see whether you cross a slab boundary
- If you are within 40–50 units of the 150-unit or 400-unit threshold, plan your charging routine accordingly — alternate-day charging may be enough to stay in your current slab
- The fixed charge and electricity duty components mean the exact rupee impact will vary; your discom's (CPDL, PSPCL, or UHBVN depending on your locality) billing calculator or a call to their helpline can give you the precise figure for your connection load

For low-speed electric scooters — those meeting the specification of 250W or less continuous motor power and a top speed of 25 km/h or less under the Central Motor Vehicles Rules — there is no RTO registration, no road tax, and no mandatory insurance to factor in. The total avoided-cost picture is meaningful, but the charging impact on your electricity bill deserves the same honest attention as the savings.
